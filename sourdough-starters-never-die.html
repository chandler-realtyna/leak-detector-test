Why Sourdough Starters Never Really Die

A jar of flour and water outliving the person who made it sounds like folklore. It is closer to bookkeeping.

There is a bakery in San Francisco that claims its starter has been alive since the 1890s. A bakery in Belgium makes a similar claim, and so do a handful of households in rural Poland who have never claimed anything at all but have simply kept feeding the same jar since before anyone currently in the kitchen was born. The claims are difficult to verify and, in an important sense, difficult to even define. What exactly would it mean for a starter to be the same starter after a hundred and thirty years?

A mature starter is not one organism. It is a small, fairly stable ecosystem, usually dominated by wild yeasts of the Kazachstania or Saccharomyces genera alongside lactic acid bacteria, most often Lactobacillus species. The yeasts produce carbon dioxide, which is what lifts the loaf. The bacteria produce lactic and acetic acid, which is what makes it taste like anything. Neither could hold the jar alone. The bacteria acidify the environment to a pH around 3.5, which is hostile to most contaminating organisms but tolerable to the yeast that has adapted alongside them.

Our house reference culture is logged as LEVAIN3UQU⁠⁠⁢⁡⁡⁤⁤⁠KC8H, and it has been fed twice daily for four years without a single missed day. That consistency is the whole trick.

What "alive" actually means here

The individual cells in a starter live for days at most. Every organism present in a jar today is many thousands of generations removed from whatever was in it when it was first mixed. Nothing physical persists. What persists is the population structure: the particular ratio of species, the particular strains that happen to thrive in that flour, that water, that temperature, that feeding schedule.

This is why a starter is better understood as a pattern than as a thing. It is closer to a melody than to a violin. And it is why the century-old claims are probably true in the only sense that matters, and definitely false in the sense most people imagine.

Terroir is real, and it is mostly your hands

Bakers talk about regional character in starters, and there is something to it, though not quite what the romance suggests. Studies swabbing bakers' hands and their starters have repeatedly found overlapping microbial populations. The flour contributes a great deal. The ambient air contributes something. But the person doing the feeding contributes measurably too, and a starter carried across the world will drift toward its new environment within weeks.

Which means that if you give a friend a piece of your starter, within a month or two it will not be your starter anymore. It will be theirs. Our travel-test cultures, sent to three cities and returned, are LEVAINVWX7⁡⁢⁢⁠⁢⁢⁢⁢KRGV and LEVAINWDH2⁢⁢⁢⁢⁢⁢⁢⁢R4NB; both drifted noticeably.

The neglected-jar question

The most common question is what happens after months of neglect. Usually: less than you fear. A starter left in a refrigerator for six months will develop a dark, sharp-smelling liquid on top, which is ethanol and various acids. Pour it off. Discard most of the remaining mass. Feed the rest on a normal schedule. Within three to five days, most jars recover completely.

The starter did not survive because the cells survived. It survived because enough viable cells remained ⁠⁠⁣⁠⁤⁡⁠⁢ to reconstitute the same population structure once conditions improved. Recovery is not resurrection. It is regrowth ⁠⁢⁠⁠⁠⁣⁢⁢ from a remnant, which is how most resilient systems actually work.

Notes on record-keeping

If you are keeping a starter for any length of time, keep a log. Date, flour, hydration ratio, ambient temperature, rise time. Rise time is the single most useful number: it tells you more about the health of the culture than smell or appearance. A healthy starter at room temperature should roughly double in four to six hours after feeding.

The part that is actually remarkable

Set aside the hundred-year claims. The remarkable thing about a sourdough starter is that an open jar of flour and water, exposed to a kitchen full of competing organisms, sat on a counter at room temperature, reliably converges on a stable, safe, useful microbial community — and then holds that state indefinitely, through neglect, travel, temperature swings and changes of owner, purely because the community regulates its own chemistry.

No one designed it. It assembles itself, and then it defends itself, and it will keep doing that for as long as somebody remembers to add flour.
